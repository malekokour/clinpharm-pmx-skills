# Context — neuroscience and CNS penetration

**Dimension:** therapeutic area · **Attaches after selection; never selected**

The area where **plasma exposure is furthest from site-of-action exposure**, and where
that gap is the central clinical-pharmacology question rather than a caveat.

## The barrier is the problem

Brain exposure is governed by passive permeability, efflux transport at the
blood–brain barrier, and plasma and brain protein binding. The consequences:

- **Plasma concentration is a weak predictor of brain concentration**, and the ratio is
  compound-specific rather than class-specific.
- The quantity that matters is **unbound brain concentration**, not total brain
  concentration and not plasma AUC.
- **Efflux transporter substrates** can have excellent permeability and negligible brain
  exposure. A permeability result alone settles nothing.
- CSF concentration is the usual accessible surrogate and is **an imperfect one** —
  useful, routinely over-interpreted.

Where the target is central, an exposure–response built on plasma should say what it is
assuming about the plasma-to-brain relationship.

## Measuring engagement

Because concentration at the target is largely inaccessible, target engagement carries
more weight here than in most areas:

- Receptor-occupancy imaging where a tracer exists — often the strongest available
  evidence for dose selection
- CSF biomarkers of pathway modulation
- Functional or electrophysiological readouts

Dose selection anchored on occupancy is common and defensible; dose selection anchored
on plasma exposure alone usually needs an explicit bridging argument.

## Placebo response and endpoint noise

Many CNS endpoints are subjective, variable, and carry large placebo responses. That
weakens empirical exposure–response and makes model-based analysis with careful
handling of placebo and dropout more important, not less.

## Populations and interactions

- **Older populations** are common, bringing polypharmacy, altered permeability, and
  comorbidity.
- **Disease itself can alter the barrier** — inflammation and neurodegeneration change
  penetration, so healthy-volunteer penetration data may not transfer to patients.
- **Interaction assessment must consider transporters at the barrier**, not only
  systemic clearance: a co-administered efflux inhibitor can raise brain exposure with
  no visible change in plasma.

That last point is the recurring miss — a negative systemic interaction study does not
exclude a central interaction.

## Safety

Central adverse effects — sedation, cognitive effects, seizure risk, suicidality
signals — often track unbound central exposure, so the same exposure-metric problem
applies to safety as to efficacy, and usually with less data.

## What this context does not do

It does not estimate brain exposure, accept an occupancy result as sufficient, or judge
a bridging argument. It stops a skill from treating plasma exposure as site-of-action
exposure without saying so.
